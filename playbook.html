Chapter 1: How markets really move

Prices move when the balance of buyers and sellers changes, and that balance changes when expectations change. Three principles carry most of the weight:

  • Surprise beats news. Markets price the consensus in advance. Good news that was expected does little; good news that beats expectations moves prices.
  • Liquidity amplifies. When money is cheap and plentiful, risky assets rise together. When it tightens, they fall together.
  • Time frame decides the story. Day-to-day moves are mostly sentiment. Over years, earnings and cash flow dominate.

Action: before reacting to any move, ask: what was expected, and what changed? Deep dive: How the Stock Market Actually Works.

Chapter 3: Valuation in plain English

Price is what you pay; value is what you get. Use at least three lenses before calling anything cheap:

  1. Earnings: P/E against peers and the company's own history; PEG to adjust for growth.
  2. Cash: free cash flow yield. Earnings can be massaged; cash is harder to fake.
  3. Balance sheet: EV/EBITDA and net debt. A low P/E with heavy debt is not a bargain.

Action: run every idea through the fair value calculator with a pessimistic, base and optimistic growth rate. If it only works in the optimistic case, pass. Deep dive: P/E Ratios and Valuation.

Chapter 4: Charts without the hype

Twenty percent of technical analysis delivers most of its value:

  • Trend: higher highs and higher lows (up) or the reverse (down). Price above the 200-day average marks a long-term uptrend.
  • Levels: support and resistance zones where buyers or sellers have repeatedly appeared.
  • Volume: breakouts on heavy volume are more trustworthy.
  • Invalidation: every chart idea needs a price that proves it wrong. That price becomes your stop.

Action: use fundamentals for what to own and charts for when and how much. Deep dive: How to Read Stock Charts.

Chapter 5: Risk and position sizing

A 50% loss needs a 100% gain to recover. Survival comes first.

  • The 1% rule: lose no more than 1–2% of your account on any single idea.
  • Stop first, size second: shares = (account × risk %) ÷ (entry − stop).
  • Demand asymmetry: aim for at least 2 : 1 reward to risk, so you can be wrong more than half the time and still come out ahead.
  • Watch correlation: five stocks in one sector are one bet, not five.

Action: never place an order without running the position size and risk/reward calculators. Deep dive: Risk Management and Position Sizing.

The pre-trade risk checklist

  1. I can explain why I'm buying in one sentence.
  2. I know the price that proves me wrong (my stop).
  3. If I'm wrong, I lose 2% of my account or less.
  4. The potential reward is at least twice the risk.
  5. I've checked for earnings or major data releases before my time horizon.
  6. This doesn't double up on a sector or theme I already own.
  7. I'm not using money I'll need within three years.
  8. I'd be comfortable holding this through a 30% market drop, or I have an exit plan.

If any answer is “no”, wait. There is always another opportunity.
