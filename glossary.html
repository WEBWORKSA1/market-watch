Market Glossary

The language of markets, decoded. Search or jump to a letter.

ABCDEFGHILMNOPQRSTUVWY

A

All-time high (ATH)
The highest price an asset has ever traded at. Breaking above it means every holder is in profit, which can reduce selling pressure.
Alpha
Return earned above a benchmark after adjusting for risk. Positive alpha suggests skill (or luck) beyond simply owning the market.
Annual percentage yield (APY)
The yearly return on savings or an investment including the effect of compounding.
Arbitrage
Profiting from a price difference for the same asset in two markets by buying in one and selling in the other simultaneously.
Ask
The lowest price a seller is currently willing to accept. Also called the offer.
Asset allocation
How a portfolio is divided among asset classes such as stocks, bonds, cash and real estate. It is the main driver of long-term risk and return.
Averaging down
Buying more of a holding after its price has fallen, lowering your average cost. It increases exposure to a position that is already losing.

B

Bear market
A decline of 20% or more from a recent high, usually accompanied by pessimism. The average bear market has been shorter than the average bull market.
Beta
A measure of how much a stock moves relative to the market. A beta of 1.5 means it has historically moved about 1.5 times as much as the index.
Bid
The highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay.
Bid-ask spread
The gap between the bid and the ask. Narrow spreads indicate liquid markets; wide spreads raise your trading cost.
Blue chip
A large, established, financially sound company with a long record of stable earnings.
Bond
A loan to a government or company that pays interest (the coupon) and returns the principal at maturity.
Book value
A company’s total assets minus total liabilities, as reported on its balance sheet.
Breakout
When price moves decisively above resistance or below support, often on higher volume.
Broker
A firm licensed to execute buy and sell orders on behalf of clients.
Bull market
A sustained rise in prices, commonly defined as a 20% gain from a low.
Buyback
When a company repurchases its own shares, reducing share count and usually boosting earnings per share.

C

Call option
A contract giving the right, not the obligation, to buy an asset at a set price before a set date.
Candlestick
A chart bar showing the open, high, low and close for a period. The body colour shows whether price closed up or down.
Capital gain
Profit from selling an asset for more than you paid. It may be taxed differently depending on how long you held it.
Capitulation
A wave of panic selling where investors give up, often near the end of a decline.
Cash flow
The cash moving in and out of a business. Operating cash flow shows what the core business generates.
Circuit breaker
An automatic trading halt triggered when an index or stock falls by a set percentage within a day.
Compound interest
Earning returns on previous returns as well as on your original money, causing growth to accelerate over time.
Consumer Price Index (CPI)
A measure of the average change in prices paid by consumers for a basket of goods and services; the headline inflation gauge.
Correction
A decline of 10% to 20% from a recent high.
Coupon
The annual interest payment on a bond, expressed as a percentage of its face value.
Cyclical stock
A company whose earnings rise and fall with the economic cycle, such as automakers, airlines and industrials.

D

Day order
An order that expires automatically if not filled by the end of the trading day.
Day trading
Buying and selling within the same day, closing all positions before the market closes.
Dead cat bounce
A brief recovery in a falling market that is followed by further declines.
Discounted cash flow (DCF)
A valuation method that estimates future cash flows and discounts them back to today at a required rate of return.
Diversification
Spreading money across many investments so that no single loss can do serious damage.
Dividend
A portion of profits paid to shareholders, usually quarterly.
Dividend yield
Annual dividends per share divided by the share price.
Dollar-cost averaging (DCA)
Investing a fixed amount at regular intervals regardless of price, buying more shares when prices are low.
Drawdown
The decline from a portfolio’s peak to its subsequent low, usually shown as a percentage.

E

Earnings per share (EPS)
Net profit divided by the number of shares outstanding.
EBITDA
Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation; a rough proxy for operating profitability.
Efficient market hypothesis
The theory that prices quickly reflect all available information, making it hard to consistently beat the market.
Exchange-traded fund (ETF)
A fund holding a basket of assets that trades on an exchange like a single stock.
Ex-dividend date
The date from which a buyer no longer receives the next declared dividend.
Expense ratio
The annual fee a fund charges, as a percentage of assets.

F

Federal funds rate
The US Federal Reserve’s target rate for overnight lending between banks; the anchor for many other interest rates.
Fiat currency
Government-issued money not backed by a physical commodity, such as the US dollar or euro.
Float
The number of shares available for public trading, excluding those held by insiders.
Forex (FX)
The global market for exchanging currencies, the largest financial market by volume.
Free cash flow
Operating cash flow minus capital expenditures: the cash a company can use for dividends, buybacks or debt reduction.
Fundamental analysis
Evaluating an investment from its financial statements, competitive position, management and economic conditions.
Futures
Contracts to buy or sell an asset at a set price on a future date, used for hedging and speculation.

G

Gap
A price jump between one period’s close and the next period’s open, often after news.
Gross domestic product (GDP)
The total value of goods and services produced by an economy over a period.
Growth stock
A company expected to grow revenue and earnings faster than average, often trading at higher valuations.
Guidance
A company’s own forecast of future revenue or earnings, often more market-moving than past results.

H

Hedge
An investment made to offset potential losses in another position.
Hedge fund
A privately offered pooled fund using a wide range of strategies, typically for accredited investors.
High-frequency trading (HFT)
Computer-driven trading that executes huge numbers of orders in fractions of a second.

I

Index
A statistical measure of a group of securities, such as the S&P 500, used as a benchmark.
Index fund
A fund designed to match the performance of an index at low cost.
Inflation
The rate at which the general level of prices rises, eroding purchasing power.
Initial public offering (IPO)
The first sale of a company’s shares to the public.
Interest rate risk
The risk that rising rates reduce the value of existing bonds and other rate-sensitive assets.
Intrinsic value
An estimate of what an asset is fundamentally worth, independent of its market price.
Inverted yield curve
When short-term bond yields exceed long-term yields; historically a recession warning sign.

L

Leverage
Using borrowed money or derivatives to increase exposure. It magnifies both gains and losses.
Limit order
An order to buy or sell only at a specified price or better.
Liquidity
How easily an asset can be bought or sold without moving its price.
Long position
Owning an asset in expectation that its price will rise.

M

Margin
Money borrowed from a broker to buy securities, using your holdings as collateral.
Margin call
A broker’s demand for more funds when account equity falls below the required minimum.
Market capitalisation
Share price multiplied by shares outstanding; the market’s value of a company.
Market maker
A firm that continuously quotes bid and ask prices, providing liquidity and earning the spread.
Market order
An order to buy or sell immediately at the best available price.
Moving average
The average price over a set number of periods, plotted as a line to smooth out noise and show trend.
Mutual fund
A pooled investment priced once per day at its net asset value.

N

Net asset value (NAV)
A fund’s assets minus liabilities, divided by shares outstanding.
Nonfarm payrolls
The monthly US report on jobs added outside agriculture; one of the most market-moving releases.

O

Options
Contracts granting the right to buy (call) or sell (put) an asset at a set price by a set date.
Order book
The live list of buy and sell orders for an asset, organised by price.
Overbought / oversold
Conditions where price has risen or fallen sharply and may be due for a pause, often gauged with RSI.

P

P/E ratio
Share price divided by earnings per share. It shows how much investors pay for each dollar of profit.
PEG ratio
The P/E ratio divided by the expected earnings growth rate; adjusts valuation for growth.
Penny stock
A very low-priced, often thinly traded share with high risk and sometimes limited disclosure.
Pip
The smallest standard price move in a currency pair, usually 0.0001.
Purchasing Managers’ Index (PMI)
A survey-based indicator of business activity; above 50 signals expansion.
Portfolio
The collection of all investments held by a person or institution.
Preferred stock
Shares that pay fixed dividends and rank ahead of common stock for payouts, usually without voting rights.
Price-to-book (P/B)
Share price divided by book value per share.
Price target
An analyst’s estimate of where a stock will trade in the future, typically over 12 months.

Q

Quantitative easing (QE)
A central bank buying bonds to lower long-term interest rates and add money to the financial system.
Quarterly earnings
The financial results public companies report every three months.

R

Rebalancing
Selling assets that have grown overweight and buying those underweight to restore a target allocation.
Recession
A significant, broad decline in economic activity lasting more than a few months.
Resistance
A price level where selling has repeatedly stopped advances.
Return on equity (ROE)
Net income divided by shareholders’ equity; a measure of profitability.
Risk tolerance
How much loss and volatility an investor can accept, financially and emotionally.
Relative Strength Index (RSI)
A momentum oscillator from 0 to 100; above 70 is often called overbought, below 30 oversold.
Rule of 72
A shortcut: 72 divided by the annual return approximates the years needed to double money.

S

Sector
A group of companies in the same industry area, such as technology, healthcare or energy.
Secondary market
Where investors trade existing securities among themselves, such as on a stock exchange.
Sharpe ratio
Excess return divided by volatility; higher means more return per unit of risk.
Short selling
Borrowing shares and selling them, hoping to buy them back cheaper later. Losses are theoretically unlimited.
Short squeeze
A rapid price rise forcing short sellers to buy back shares, pushing the price higher still.
Small cap
A company with a relatively small market value, commonly between about $300 million and $2 billion.
Spread
The difference between two prices or rates, such as bid and ask, or two bond yields.
Stablecoin
A cryptocurrency designed to hold a stable value, usually pegged to the US dollar.
Stock split
Dividing each share into multiple shares, lowering the price per share without changing total value.
Stop-loss order
An order that becomes a market order once a set price is reached, used to limit losses.
Support
A price level where buying has repeatedly stopped declines.
Swing trading
Holding positions for days to weeks to capture a single price swing.

T

Technical analysis
Studying price, volume and chart patterns to judge likely future moves.
Ticker symbol
The short code identifying a security on an exchange, such as AAPL.
Total return
Price change plus dividends or interest, the true measure of investment performance.
Treasury
Debt issued by the US government: bills (short-term), notes (2–10 years) and bonds (20–30 years).
Trailing stop
A stop-loss that moves up with price by a set amount or percentage, locking in gains.

U

Underweight / overweight
Holding less or more of an asset than its weight in a benchmark; also used as analyst ratings.
Unrealised gain
A profit on paper on a position you still hold.

V

Value stock
A company trading at a low price relative to its earnings, cash flow or assets.
VIX
An index of expected 30-day S&P 500 volatility derived from option prices; nicknamed the “fear gauge”.
Volatility
How much and how quickly prices move, often measured by standard deviation.
Volume
The number of shares or contracts traded in a period.

W

Wash sale
Selling at a loss and rebuying the same or a substantially identical security within a short window; tax rules may disallow the loss.
Whale
An individual or entity holding enough of an asset to move its price, a term common in crypto.

Y

Yield
The income return on an investment, such as interest or dividends, as a percentage of price.
Yield curve
A chart of bond yields across maturities. Its slope reflects expectations for growth, inflation and interest rates.
Year-to-date (YTD)
Performance from the first trading day of the current year to today.
