8 Economic Indicators That Move Markets

Market.Watch Editorial TeamUpdated October 31, 20269 min read

Some of the biggest daily swings in stocks, bonds and currencies happen at precisely scheduled moments: when government agencies publish economic data. Knowing what each release measures, and why markets care, turns a confusing jolt into an understandable reaction. Keep our economic calendar open as you read.

The golden rule: it’s the surprise that matters

Before each release, economists publish a consensus forecast. Prices already reflect that forecast. When the actual number arrives, markets move on the gap between actual and expected. A “strong” jobs report that merely matches expectations may barely move prices; a modest one that badly misses can trigger a sharp sell-off.

1. Consumer Price Index (CPI)

CPI tracks the prices households pay for a broad basket of goods and services. Markets focus on core CPI, which excludes volatile food and energy. Higher-than-expected inflation raises the odds that the central bank keeps rates high or raises them, which typically pushes bond yields and the currency up and weighs on rate-sensitive growth stocks. Since the inflation surge of the early 2020s, CPI day has become one of the most volatile days of the month.

2. PCE Price Index

The Personal Consumption Expenditures index is the US Federal Reserve’s preferred inflation measure. It covers a wider range of spending than CPI and adjusts as consumers substitute between products. Because CPI arrives earlier, PCE surprises are usually smaller, but it directly shapes the Fed’s projections.

3. Nonfarm payrolls and the jobs report

Released on the first Friday of most months, the US employment report includes jobs added, the unemployment rate and average hourly earnings. Strong job growth signals a healthy economy, good for corporate profits, but also potential wage-driven inflation and higher rates. That tension means stocks can react either way; bonds and the dollar usually react more predictably.

4. Central-bank rate decisions

The Federal Reserve, European Central Bank, Bank of England, Bank of Japan, Bank of Canada, Reserve Bank of India and others set short-term policy rates roughly six to eight times a year. The decision itself is often fully expected. The market-moving information is in the statement, the press conference and, for the Fed, the “dot plot” of officials’ rate projections. A hawkish tone (worried about inflation, inclined to keep rates high) tends to lift the currency and yields; a dovish tone (worried about growth, inclined to cut) tends to lift stocks and bonds.

5. Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

GDP measures total economic output and is reported quarterly, usually as an annualised growth rate, with later revisions. Two consecutive negative quarters are a common informal definition of recession, though official recession dating uses broader evidence. Because GDP looks backward and is heavily anticipated by other data, it moves markets less than you might expect.

6. Purchasing Managers’ Indexes (PMI)

PMIs survey purchasing managers about new orders, output, employment, supplier deliveries and inventories. A reading above 50 signals expansion; below 50, contraction. Because they are timely and forward-looking, PMIs are watched closely for turning points, especially manufacturing PMIs in China, the eurozone and the US.

7. Retail sales

Consumer spending drives roughly two-thirds of the US economy. Monthly retail sales show whether households are still spending. Strong numbers lift consumer-discretionary stocks and the currency; weak numbers raise growth fears. Watch the “control group”, which strips out volatile categories and feeds directly into GDP estimates.

8. Weekly jobless claims

Every Thursday, the US reports new filings for unemployment benefits. It is the most frequent read on the labour market. A sustained rise in claims is one of the earliest signs that layoffs are spreading, which can push bond yields lower as markets anticipate rate cuts.

Using the calendar in practice

  • Check the week’s high-importance events every Monday.
  • Avoid entering new short-term trades minutes before a major release unless that is your strategy; spreads widen and moves can reverse.
  • Long-term investors can mostly ignore the noise, but understanding it helps you avoid panic decisions on volatile days.
