Compound Interest and Dollar-Cost Averaging

Market.Watch Editorial TeamUpdated October 31, 20267 min read

Two ideas do more for ordinary investors than any stock tip: compounding, which makes money grow faster the longer you leave it, and dollar-cost averaging, which makes investing regularly a habit instead of a guessing game. Together they explain why consistent savers often end up wealthier than clever traders.

How compounding works

Compounding means earning returns on your past returns. Invest $10,000 at 7% a year: after one year you have $10,700. In year two you earn 7% on $10,700, not $10,000, so you gain $749 instead of $700. The extra seems trivial at first. Over decades it dominates.

Notice that the last ten years add more than the first thirty combined. That is the shape of compounding: slow, then sudden.

The Rule of 72

To estimate how long money takes to double, divide 72 by the annual return. At 6%, about 12 years; at 9%, about 8 years; at 12%, about 6 years. It also works for costs: at 3% inflation, prices roughly double every 24 years. Try it in our Rule of 72 calculator.

Why starting early beats investing more

Consider two investors earning 7% a year. Alex invests $300 a month from age 25 to 35, then stops: $36,000 in total. Sam starts at 35 and invests $300 a month until 65: $108,000 in total. At 65, Alex’s smaller, earlier contributions have grown to roughly as much as Sam’s, in some scenarios more, despite investing one-third as much money. The extra decade of compounding does the heavy lifting. Run your own version in the compound growth calculator.

The enemies of compounding

  • Fees: a 1% annual fee can consume a quarter or more of your ending wealth over 30 years.
  • Inflation: always think in real (after-inflation) returns. See the inflation calculator.
  • Taxes: tax-advantaged retirement accounts let compounding run without annual drag.
  • Large losses: a 50% loss needs a 100% gain to recover. Avoiding catastrophic drawdowns matters more than chasing the highest return.
  • Interruptions: selling in panic and sitting out the recovery breaks the chain.

Dollar-cost averaging (DCA)

DCA means investing a fixed amount at regular intervals, say $500 every month, regardless of price. When prices are high your $500 buys fewer shares; when prices fall it buys more. Over time, your average cost per share ends up below the average price over the period, because you automatically bought more of the cheap shares.

DCA vs. lump sum

If you already have a large sum, research has generally found that investing it all at once has beaten spreading it out more often than not, simply because markets rise more often than they fall. But lump-sum investing carries the regret risk of buying right before a decline. DCA trades a little expected return for a smoother emotional ride. For most people investing from each paycheque, the question is moot: regular investing is DCA.

Making it automatic

  1. Pick a low-cost, diversified fund (see ETFs and index funds).
  2. Set up an automatic monthly transfer right after payday.
  3. Increase the amount each time your income rises.
  4. Review once or twice a year, and rebalance if needed. Ignore the daily noise.

Key takeaways

  • Compounding rewards time above all: start as early as you can.
  • Protect compounding from fees, taxes, inflation and big losses.
  • Dollar-cost averaging builds discipline and lowers average cost in volatile markets.
  • Automation beats willpower.
